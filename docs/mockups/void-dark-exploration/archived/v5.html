The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Incremental Rewrites

Every team eventually faces a codebase that fights back. The instinct is to start fresh. That instinct is almost always wrong.

There is a particular kind of despair that sets in around year three of a software project. The codebase has grown organically, shaped by deadlines and compromises. The original authors have moved on. New developers spend more time reading code than writing it, and what they read makes less sense each week.

The conversation in the team chat always follows the same arc. Someone shares a particularly gnarly piece of legacy code. Others pile on with their own examples. Eventually, someone says the thing everyone has been thinking: "We should just rewrite it."

It feels like clarity. Like the grown-up decision. The current codebase is a mess, so we will build the next one the right way, with everything we have learned. We will use better tools, better patterns, better architecture. We will write tests this time.

This is one of the most seductive ideas in software engineering. It is also, in most cases, a catastrophic mistake.

Why Big Rewrites Fail

Joel Spolsky wrote about this in 2000, and his argument has only gotten stronger with time. The fundamental problem with a rewrite is not technical. It is economic. A rewrite means you are spending months or years building something that, at best, will do exactly what the current system already does. You are running in place while your competitors move forward.

But there are deeper problems than just the opportunity cost:

  • You lose embedded knowledge. That ugly conditional on line 347? It handles a billing edge case that took three weeks to diagnose in production. When you rewrite, you lose these hard-won patches unless you understand every single one of them, which you almost certainly do not.
  • The estimate is always wrong. Rewrites are estimated based on building the system you wish you had, not the system you actually need. The real system has hundreds of edge cases, integrations, and workarounds that only reveal themselves during implementation.
  • Team morale follows a predictable curve. Initial excitement gives way to the slow realization that you are solving the same problems again, just with newer syntax. By month six, the rewrite team is as frustrated as they were before, but now they are also behind schedule.
  • You are rewriting a moving target. The old system does not freeze while you rewrite. Bug fixes, feature requests, and customer needs keep flowing in, and someone has to maintain both systems simultaneously.

The Incremental Alternative

The alternative is less dramatic but far more effective: change the system piece by piece, while it continues to run. This is not a new idea. Martin Fowler calls it the Strangler Fig Pattern, named after the tropical plants that gradually envelop and replace their host trees.

The core principle is simple. Instead of replacing the entire system at once, you identify a boundary, build the new version of that piece, redirect traffic to it, and retire the old piece. Then you repeat.

Finding the Right Boundaries

Not every part of a system is equally painful, and not every boundary is equally clean. The art of incremental rewriting is choosing where to cut. Good candidates share a few characteristics:

  1. They have a well-defined interface, even if the implementation behind it is messy.
  2. They cause disproportionate maintenance burden relative to their complexity.
  3. They can be validated independently. You need to know if the new version works before you retire the old one.
  4. They do not require coordinated changes across many other components.
Note: If you cannot find a single component that meets these criteria, that itself is valuable information. It usually means your system lacks clear boundaries, and the first step is introducing them, not rewriting code.

The Strangler in Practice

Here is a concrete example. Say you have a monolithic application with an authentication system baked into the request lifecycle. The auth code is scattered across middleware, controllers, and even some database queries. It is the kind of thing that makes you want to burn it all down.

Instead, you introduce a thin proxy layer:

// New auth middleware — runs alongside the old one
async function verifyAuth(req, res, next) {
  const newResult = await newAuthService.verify(req);
  const oldResult = await legacyAuth.verify(req);

  // Compare results in shadow mode
  if (newResult.userId !== oldResult.userId) {
    metrics.increment('auth.mismatch');
    logger.warn('Auth mismatch', { newResult, oldResult });
  }

  // Still use old result until confidence is high
  req.user = oldResult;
  next();
}

You run both systems in parallel, comparing results. When the new system has handled millions of requests without a mismatch, you flip the switch. If something goes wrong, you flip it back. At no point is the system down. At no point are you asking your team to take a leap of faith.

The Psychological Dimension

There is a reason incremental rewrites feel less satisfying than starting fresh. They require you to spend time in the codebase you already hate. You have to read the ugly code, understand it, and carefully replace it without breaking anything. It is meticulous, unglamorous work.

The programmer, like the poet, works only slightly removed from pure thought-stuff. He builds castles in the air, from air, creating by exertion of the imagination.

Fred Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month

Brooks understood something essential about our profession. We are drawn to creation, not maintenance. A rewrite lets us create. An incremental migration asks us to garden, to tend, to patiently cultivate. It requires a different kind of discipline.

But here is the thing: the discipline pays off. Not just in the final result, but in what you learn along the way. When you incrementally rewrite a system, you develop a deep understanding of what it actually does, not what you think it does, not what the documentation says it does, but what it actually does in production under load with real users doing unexpected things.

When a Rewrite Is Actually Right

I am not arguing that rewrites are never appropriate. There are legitimate cases:

The technology is genuinely dead
If your application is written in a language or framework with no community, no security patches, and no available developers, you may have no choice. But this is rarer than people claim.
The domain has fundamentally changed
If your product started as a simple CRUD application and now needs to be a real-time collaborative platform, the core architecture may be genuinely incompatible with the new requirements.
The codebase is tiny
If you can rewrite the entire system in a week, the risk calculus is different. The danger of rewrites scales with size.

A Framework for the Decision

When the rewrite conversation comes up on your team, and it will, here is a quick litmus test:

Signal Points to
Pain is concentrated in specific modules Incremental rewrite
Pain is distributed everywhere Introduce boundaries first
Team wants new technology Incremental migration
Core data model is wrong Consider rewrite
System is less than 10k lines Rewrite might be fine
System is mission-critical Never big-bang rewrite

The Compound Effect

The real magic of incremental rewrites is not any single step. It is the compound effect over time. Each piece you replace makes the next piece easier to replace. Boundaries become clearer. Tests accumulate. The team builds confidence.

Six months into an incremental rewrite, you have a system that is measurably better, still running in production, and fully understood by the team. Six months into a big rewrite, you have a prototype that handles the happy path and a growing list of edge cases you had not thought of.

Making It Work on Your Team

If you want to advocate for incremental rewrites on your team, here is practical advice:

Start with a quick win. Find the smallest, most painful component and replace it. Nothing builds momentum like demonstrating that the approach works. Pick something visible so the rest of the team can see the improvement.

Measure everything. Before you touch any code, establish baselines. Response times, error rates, deployment frequency, time to resolve incidents. You need numbers, not feelings, to justify continued investment.

Document the boundaries. As you carve out components, write down where the old system ends and the new one begins. This map is one of the most valuable artifacts you will produce. Future developers will thank you.

Resist the urge to gold-plate. The goal of each increment is to replace the old implementation, not to build the perfect implementation. You can improve it later. Right now, focus on parity and correctness.

The best code I have ever worked with was not written in a burst of inspiration. It was shaped over years by developers who cared enough to improve it incrementally, who chose the patient work of making things better over the seductive promise of making things new.

That is the unsexy truth about software engineering. The systems that endure are not the ones that were designed perfectly from the start. They are the ones that were tended carefully, piece by piece, by people who understood that good software is grown, not built.
